Adjusted EBITDA

Start with net profit. Add back your salary, interest, depreciation, amortization, and one-time expenses. That's your adjusted EBITDA - the number buyers actually care about.

Example: $600K net profit + $250K owner salary + $50K one-time legal fees = $900K adjusted EBITDA

Industry Multiple

Your EBITDA gets multiplied by a number that depends on your industry, size, and risk profile. Here are the current ranges for service businesses:

Risk Adjustments

This is where most owners get surprised. Buyers don't just look at your revenue - they stress-test everything that could go wrong after they write the check.

  • Owner dependency - Can it run without you for 4 weeks?
  • Customer concentration - Do your top 3 clients make up more than 30% of revenue?
  • Recurring revenue - What percentage is on contract vs. one-time?
  • Employee retention - Will your key people stay post-sale?
  • Contract backlog - How much future revenue is locked in?

Why 73% of Business Owners Get a Lower Price Than Expected

According to the International Business Brokers Association, nearly three-quarters of business owners are disappointed by the offers they receive. The gap between what they think their business is worth and what a buyer will actually pay comes down to three fixable problems.

Problem 1: Owner Dependency

If you are the business - if every big decision, every key client relationship, every crisis runs through you - a buyer sees a ticking clock. The day you leave, value walks out the door.

Buyers call this "key person risk." It's the single biggest multiple killer in service businesses. A company that can run for 4 weeks without the owner commands a 1.5-2x higher multiple than one that can't.

Per the Exit Planning Institute, businesses with documented processes and a strong #2 leader sell for 20-40% more than owner-dependent equivalents.

Problem 2: Customer Concentration

If your top 3 clients represent more than 30% of revenue, buyers will discount your price - or walk away entirely. One lost contract shouldn't tank the business.

The fix isn't complicated. It's deliberate. Diversify your client base over 18-24 months before going to market. Buyers want to see no single client above 10-15% of revenue.

Problem 3: No Recurring Revenue

Project-based revenue is worth less than contract revenue. Period. A plumbing company doing $8M in one-off jobs trades at 4x. The same company with 30% recurring maintenance contracts trades at 6x. That's a $16M difference on an $8M business.

Buyers pay more for predictability. If you can show contracted, recurring revenue, you're speaking their language.

The 10-Minute Exercise That Shows You Exactly Where You Stand

You can spend $15,000 on a formal valuation. Or you can spend 10 minutes answering three questions that tell you 80% of what you need to know.

  1. What's your adjusted EBITDA?
    Net profit + owner salary + interest + depreciation + one-time costs. If you don't know this number off the top of your head, that's the first problem.
  2. What percentage of your revenue is recurring?
    Maintenance contracts, service agreements, retainers. Below 20% and you're leaving serious money on the table.
  3. Could your business run for 4 weeks without you?
    If the honest answer is no, your multiple is getting discounted by 20-40%. That's real money.

If you scored well on all three, you're ahead of most owners. If not, the good news: every one of these is fixable in 12-18 months.
